# Editorial context

Use this guide for blog posts, articles, reports, essays, polished meeting summaries, and long-form updates.

## Goal

Give the text a point of view, rhythm, and shape without turning it into promotional filler. Editorial writing can have more voice than documentation, but every paragraph still needs work to do.

## Build around a real center

Start from the concrete material: a decision, incident, data point, story, observation, or tension. Do not begin with generic scene-setting like "In today's fast-paced world."

Use the introduction to orient the reader, not to overstate importance.

Develop ideas with examples. If the text makes a claim, give the reader enough material to judge it.

## Let rhythm vary

Mix short and longer sentences. Use paragraph breaks to manage pace. Let a paragraph be one sentence if that gives the point room.

Use questions sparingly. They should open a real issue, not simulate engagement.

## Avoid soft endings

Do not end by restating the introduction. End with the strongest remaining fact, consequence, question, or next action.

## Patterns

Bad: "This initiative represents a crucial milestone in our ongoing journey toward operational excellence."

Good: "The new process removes two approval steps. The first team to try it shipped a patch in 40 minutes instead of half a day."

Bad: "The future of this technology is bright, and its potential is limitless."

Good: "The next test is harder: run the same workflow across three legacy systems without adding a manual fallback."

Bad: "The workshop was engaging, insightful, and productive."

Good: "The workshop changed one decision. We will keep the batch import for pension customers until the API handles partial failures."

## Editorial self-check

1. Does the opening start from real material?
2. Is there a clear through-line?
3. Are claims supported by examples, data, or concrete observations?
4. Does the text avoid generic importance language?
5. Does the ending add something instead of summarizing?
