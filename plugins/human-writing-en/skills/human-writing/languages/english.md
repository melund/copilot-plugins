# English writing guide

Use this guide for English prose that should read as human-authored rather than AI-generated. The aim is not to make every text casual. The aim is to make the writing specific, situated, and believable for its reader.

## High-risk vocabulary

LLMs overuse certain words. One or two may be fine, but clusters are a strong signal.

### Hard bans in user-facing prose

- great question, excellent point, i hope this helps, let me know if you'd like
- as of my last training update, based on my training data

### Words to watch

Avoid these unless the context truly requires them:

- additionally, moreover, furthermore, consequently, therefore, ultimately, notably
- delve, foster, leverage, utilize, underscore, garner, showcase, enhance
- pivotal, crucial, vital, intricate, vibrant, seamless, robust, dynamic, innovative, groundbreaking
- tapestry, landscape as metaphor, testament, interplay, synergy, paradigm
- holistic, cutting-edge, game-changer, realm, journey as metaphor
- nestled, bustling, in the heart of, breathtaking, renowned
- it's important to note, it's worth mentioning, at the end of the day
- in today's anything, in the world of, in the realm of, in a nutshell
- moving forward, going forward, with that being said, needless to say
- when it comes to, a significant number of, last but not least
- serves as, stands as, boasts meaning "has", aligns with
- exemplifies, epitomizes, embodies, encapsulates
- metaphorical navigate or navigating, elevate, embark, unleash, uncover
- rich, profound, comprehensive, extensive as filler adjectives

### Direct replacements

- Use "is" instead of "serves as" or "stands as."
- Use "has" instead of "boasts."
- Use "shows" instead of "showcases."
- Use "use" instead of "leverage" or "utilize."
- Use "try" instead of "endeavor."
- Use "start" instead of "embark."
- Use "look into" instead of "delve into."
- Use "to" instead of "in order to."
- Use "because" instead of "due to the fact that."
- Use "now" instead of "at this point in time."
- Use "can" instead of "has the ability to."
- Use "if" instead of "in the event that."

## Sentence patterns to avoid

### Significance inflation

Write the fact. Skip the commentary about its importance. If something is important, the fact itself should show that.

Bad: "The institute was established in 1989, marking a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional statistics."

Good: "The institute was established in 1989 to collect and publish regional statistics."

### Notability name-dropping

Do not list major outlets, awards, or follower counts to imply authority without substance.

Bad: "Her views were cited by NYT, BBC, FT, and others, and she has 500,000 followers."

Good: "In a 2024 New York Times interview, she argued for outcome-based AI regulation."

### Superficial -ing phrases

Cut trailing participle phrases that add fake depth, such as "highlighting its importance," "underscoring the need for," and "reflecting broader trends."

Bad: "The color palette resonates with the region's natural beauty, symbolizing Texas bluebonnets and the Gulf coast, reflecting the community's deep connection to the land."

Good: "The architect chose blue, green, and gold to reference local bluebonnets and the Gulf coast."

### The "despite challenges" formula

Do not end with generic resilience language. Name the challenge and the evidence.

Bad: "Despite its industrial prosperity, Korattur faces challenges typical of urban areas. Despite these challenges, it continues to thrive."

Good: "Traffic increased after three IT parks opened in 2015. The city began a drainage project in 2022 to address recurring floods."

### Negative parallelisms

Use "not just X, but also Y" and "it's not about X, it's about Y" sparingly.

Bad: "It's not just about autocomplete; it's about unlocking creativity at scale."

Good: "These tools do more than autocomplete. They generate whole functions and test scaffolding."

### Rule of three

Do not manufacture a third item to make a sentence feel complete. If there are two things, say two. If there are four, say four.

Bad: "The event features keynote sessions, panel discussions, and networking opportunities."

Good: "The event includes talks and panels. There is time for informal networking between sessions."

### Elegant variation

Do not cycle through synonyms to avoid repetition. Repetition is often clearer.

Bad: "The protagonist faces challenges. The main character must overcome obstacles. The central figure eventually triumphs."

Good: "The protagonist faces challenges but eventually triumphs and returns home."

### False ranges

Avoid "from X to Y" unless X and Y are meaningful endpoints on a scale.

Bad: "The book covers topics from the Big Bang to dark matter."

Good: "The book covers the Big Bang, star formation, and dark matter theories."

## Punctuation and formatting

Avoid semicolons in most user-facing prose. Use a period, or use a comma with a conjunction.

Avoid em dashes. LLMs overuse them where humans would use commas, parentheses, or separate sentences. If one is genuinely useful, use at most one per 500 words.

Use sentence case for headings: "Strategic negotiations and global partnerships," not "Strategic Negotiations And Global Partnerships."

Use straight quotes and apostrophes, not curly ones.

Do not bold phrases for emphasis in a "key takeaways" style. Bold only for structure or defined terms.

Avoid bullet lists where every item starts with a bold label and colon unless the requested format calls for it.

Do not use emoji unless the user asks for them.

## Voice

Match register before adding personality:

- Technical docs: neutral, concrete, low emotion.
- Work emails: direct, polite, specific.
- Marketing copy: persuasive but factual.
- Academic writing: careful claims and explicit evidence.

When the user provides a writing sample, mirror that tone unless they ask for a change.

## English self-check

1. Did I use three or more high-risk vocabulary words?
2. Do multiple sentences end with superficial -ing phrases?
3. Is every sentence the same length?
4. Did I use "serves as," "stands as," or "boasts"?
5. Does any paragraph inflate importance without evidence?
6. Did I end with a vague positive conclusion?
7. Are there em dashes or semicolons?
8. Did I force a list of three?
9. Did I add vague notability instead of concrete evidence?
10. Did I preserve the right register for the reader?
